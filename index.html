Oscar Wilde's London

In the autumn of 1879 a young Irishman with a first in Greats and very little money took rooms off the Strand. In the spring of 1897 he walked out of Pentonville and took the night boat to Dieppe. This map follows the city between those two dates: the theatres and the slums, the salons and the courts, the music halls, the scandals and the river.

The city that made Oscar Wilde

"This book is not about Oscar Wilde," begins Wolf Von Eckardt, Sander Gilman and J. Edward Chamberlin's Oscar Wilde's London (1987). It is about the city around him. Their book is a scrapbook of vices and virtues: Aestheticism and Morris wallpaper, Irving at the Lyceum and Marie Lloyd in the halls, Booth's poverty maps and the Ripper, Jumbo the elephant and W. G. Grace, Stead's "Maiden Tribute" and the Cleveland Street scandal.

This site puts their London on a map. Every place below cites the pages of the book it draws on. Where the book is silent, as it often is about Wilde's own movements and about queer London, the entry draws on scholarship and names its sources. Where the book gets something wrong, the entry says so (see "Where the book slips").

      About the book, and this map

      The source

      Wolf Von Eckardt, Sander L. Gilman and J. Edward Chamberlin, Oscar Wilde's London: A Scrapbook of Vices and Virtues, 1880–1900 (1987). An urban critic, a cultural historian and a literary scholar wrote it as twelve themed chapters, from "Lilies and Sunflowers" to "Juliets of a Night", with hundreds of period pictures.

      This map was built by reading the whole book from its scanned text and recording every London place it names, with the printed page number. Where an entry says "the book", it means this one. Numbers in the Sources line of each entry are its pages.

      Beyond the book

      The book says little about Wilde's own movements or about queer London. Those entries draw on Wilde scholarship (Ellmann, Sturgis, Holland, Bristow, the Complete Letters), the history of sexuality (Cook, Kaplan, Upchurch, Weeks, Walkowitz) and histories of London (White, Fishman, Koven, Rappaport). They are cited as further reading. A digitised archive of 1895 press reports settled one disagreement over the trial evidence.
